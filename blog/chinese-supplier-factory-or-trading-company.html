How to Tell If a Chinese Supplier Is Actually a Factory (and Why It Sometimes Doesn't Matter)

11 September 2026 · 7 min read · Jun Lee

The signals worth checking when you want to know whether a Chinese supplement supplier is a manufacturer or a trading company — and when the answer genuinely changes what you should do.

"Is this a real factory or a trading company?"

It is the first question most supplement buyers ask, and it is usually asked in a way that assumes a binary answer with a clear good side. In practice the Chinese supplement supply chain has more shapes than that, and the shape that matters depends on what you are buying.

Here is what the landscape actually contains, what signals are worth checking, and — more usefully — when the answer should change your decision.

The shapes you will encounter

ShapeWhat it isTypical behaviour
Contract manufacturer with own linesProduces on premises, may also formulateCares about batch size, format fit, scheduling
Group manufacturer with multiple sitesSeveral plants under one groupQuotes you the group, produces on one site
Trading companyBuys from manufacturers, sells onFast, flexible, often better English; margin sits in the price
Trading company with a partial stakeOwns part of a plant or a lineMixed — sometimes excellent, sometimes confusing accountability
Agent / sourcing officeNot the seller at all, coordinatesUseful for small buyers, weakest on accountability
Brand owner using a contract manufacturerHas the brand, outsources productionWill present as the sourcing party; production is elsewhere
Integrated raw material + finished goods producerMakes some ingredients, blends othersStrong on its own ingredients, not on the rest

None of this is a scandal. It is what a mature contract manufacturing market looks like — the same variation you find in Europe or North America, just with the language barrier making it harder to detect.

Why buyers ask, and what they are actually worried about

Strip the question back and there are usually five underlying worries:

  1. Price transparency — "am I paying factory price or factory price plus an invisible margin?"
  2. Accountability — "if something goes wrong, who is responsible and who can I actually pressure?"
  3. Sub-tier exposure — "if they subcontract, I have no idea who is really making my product"
  4. Control — "can they change ingredient sources without telling me?"
  5. IP and tooling — "if I pay for a mould or a formulation, who owns it?"

Being clear about which of these you care about most is more useful than getting a label. A trading company can be perfectly fine on 1 and 4, weak on 2 and 3, and a disaster on 5. A factory can be strong on 2 and 5 and still subcontract your specific process to a third party.

Signals worth checking

These are evidence-based checks, not assumptions. None is conclusive on its own.

Process-specific answers

Ask questions that require proximity to production rather than access to a catalogue. A capable manufacturing contact can answer these without hesitating; someone reading from a price list cannot.

  • What is your blending capacity per batch for this dosage form?
  • What is your typical yield loss on this format, and what drives the variation?
  • How do you handle a raw material that fails incoming QC — reject, return, or hold?
  • Which steps do you run in house, and which do you subcontract?
  • What runs on this line the week before and after my batch, and does changeover matter here?
  • What would make you turn this project down?

That last question is the most diagnostic one on the list. Manufacturing people who know their plant will tell you about constraints. A supplier who agrees to everything either does not know the plant or is not planning to hold the price.

Licence and registration scope

The business licence (营业执照) shows registered scope, and the food production licence (食品生产许可证, with its "SC" number) shows which food categories a site is licensed to produce. Health foods (保健食品) sit in a separate regime again.

What matters is the match between the licence in front of you and the product you are buying. A licence that covers a different category than your product is not a moral failing — it is a reason to ask who actually holds the licence for your category.

Certificates read for the holder's name

Certificates are the most misread documents in sourcing. Ask yourself, for every certificate you are shown:

  • Whose name is on it?
  • Which site address does it cover?
  • What scope does it actually state?
  • When was it issued, and is it still valid?

A certificate in a parent company's name covering a different site is not evidence about your batch. That is not a trick — it is simply the wrong document for the question you are asking.

Site evidence

The strongest single piece of evidence is seeing production. A live video walkthrough of the specific line — not a marketing video — or an on-site visit answers more than a folder of documents. If a supplier resists a live walkthrough of the line that will run your batch, that resistance is itself information.

Behavioural signals

Less discussed, often more predictive:

  • Do they ask about your target market before quoting?
  • Do they ask for a specification, or quote from a product name?
  • Do they volunteer what they cannot do?
  • Is the MOQ stable when you ask twice, and across two conversations?
  • Do they answer questions in writing, or only on calls?

When "it's a trading company" genuinely matters

Not all cases are equal. These are the situations where the distinction should actually change your plan.

You need to own tooling or IP. If you are paying for a mould, a proprietary blend or a private formulation, the entity you contract with must be able to hold and transfer that ownership. This is the area where the least structure exists and the most damage is done.

Your product depends on a specific process. If your format requires a particular technology — a specific film-forming system, a particular release profile — and the trading company will not name the plant, you cannot assess whether that process is genuinely available or whether it is being described optimistically.

You need change control. If your market requires that you know when an ingredient source changes, a supply chain with invisible layers makes that unenforceable. You cannot audit a supplier you have not been told about.

You need direct accountability. For high-volume or regulated projects, an unbroken line to the entity that runs the line is worth paying for.

When it genuinely doesn't matter

Small batches and early-stage validation. If you need 500 units to test a market, a good trading company with multi-format flexibility may be a better partner than a factory that normally runs 50,000-unit batches. Aggregation is a real service.

Multi-format or multi-ingredient projects. A single small factory rarely does capsules, gummies and sachets well. An intermediary who coordinates several specialists may get you a better product than forcing everything through one plant.

You want English-language project management. It is a genuinely scarce skill in the supply chain, and coordinating it is work that someone should be paid for. The question is whether you know you are paying for it.

Exploration. Before you have a defined product, breadth of access is more valuable than depth of one relationship.

The honest framing is this: a trading company is not a problem. An undisclosed trading company is a problem. What you are buying is the ability to make an accurate decision, and that requires knowing which party is which.

How to find out without making it awkward

Asking "are you a factory or a trading company?" directly tends to produce a defensive answer. These alternatives work better:

  1. Ask about the site, not the status. "Which site would produce this, and can you walk me through that line live?"
  2. Ask about the licence. "Which entity holds the production licence for this category, and can I see it?"
  3. Ask about subcontracting openly. "Which parts of this would you do in house?" Framed as a planning question, this is not accusatory — it is what a serious buyer asks.
  4. Ask who signs the contract and who receives payment. If those differ, ask why in writing.
  5. Ask what they would refuse. Constraints reveal familiarity.

If the answers are coherent and the documents line up with them, you have what you need. If they are coherent but the documents point elsewhere, you have found the most valuable thing a verification process produces: a specific question to resolve before money moves.

The practical conclusion

Stop asking whether your supplier is a factory. Start asking three narrower questions:

  • Who is legally responsible for the batch I am buying?
  • Where physically will it be produced, and by whom?
  • Can I see evidence for both that is specific to my product?

Those three questions survive every structural variation of the Chinese supply chain, and they are answerable. The label is not — and it was never the thing you needed.
