How to Verify a COA From a Chinese Supplement Supplier

2026-09-05

Batch correspondence, test method, issuing laboratory and specification limits — the four things a certificate of analysis must tell you, and what to do when it doesn't.

A certificate of analysis is the document buyers lean on most and understand least. It arrives as a clean one-page PDF with a table of results, a batch number and a signature, and it produces a feeling of reassurance that is roughly proportional to how official it looks rather than to what it actually establishes.

This article is about reading a COA properly — and about the specific questions a COA cannot answer, which is where most sourcing risk actually lives.

What a COA is

A certificate of analysis is a supplier's written statement about the results of testing performed on a specific batch of material, measured against a specification.

It is a record of testing. It is not a certification, not an approval, not a guarantee of identity, and not a statement about the batch you will receive unless the batch number connects the two.

That last point is where most of the value of this article sits, so let's start there.

The four things a usable COA must tell you

If any one of these four is missing, the document cannot do its job.

1. Batch correspondence

The COA must name a batch or lot number, and that number must be the number on the goods or on the packing list for the shipment you are receiving.

This is the single most common and most consequential gap. Buyers routinely accept a COA from a previous batch — sometimes called a "typical" or "representative" COA — and then treat it as evidence about their order. It is not. It is evidence about a different batch of material.

Ask for the batch number on the COA to be confirmed against the shipment documentation, in writing. If the answer is that the COA for your exact batch will be issued after production, that is reasonable — agree the timing, and hold the release of the final payment against it.

2. Test method

For each parameter, the COA should state the method used. "Assay: 98.2%" without a method is a number without a meaning.

Methods are not equivalent, and the differences are large:

Method typeWhat it measuresPractical implication
HPLC / UPLCSeparates and quantifies the specific compoundClosest to a true assay for most actives
GCVolatiles, fatty acids, residual solventsStandard for many oils and some actives
UV-Vis spectrophotometryAbsorbance, often calibrated to a standardFaster and cheaper; can overstate purity if interfering compounds absorb
TitrationReacts the analyteFine for some minerals and acids; not specific for complex extracts
MicrobiologicalCounts or growth-basedAppropriate for probiotics and microbial limits
Gravimetric / loss on dryingMass-basedInformative but not an identity test

A specification written as "≥98% by UV" and one written as "≥98% by HPLC" describe materially different materials at materially different costs. If your quotation compares two suppliers on "98%", and one of them is measuring by UV, you are not comparing like with like.

For botanical extracts, you also need to know what is being standardised — the marker compound, the ratio, or something else — because "10:1 extract" and "standardised to 20% marker" are different products.

3. Issuing laboratory and its standing

Who ran the test? An in-house QC laboratory, an affiliated laboratory, or an independent third party all carry different weight.

In China, laboratory accreditation commonly appears as CNAS (China National Accreditation Service) and/or CMA (China Metrology Accreditation). Where a laboratory holds accreditation to ISO/IEC 17025, the scope of that accreditation matters — a lab can be accredited for some methods and not others.

This is checkable, and it is worth checking for any parameter you are relying on commercially.

4. Specification limits alongside results

A result is only meaningful against the limit it is being compared to. A COA should show the specification range and the measured result side by side, plus a pass/fail conclusion.

Watch for COAs that show results with no limits, or that state "complies" without showing the numeric result. Both are unverifiable. You cannot audit a conclusion that has no underlying number.

Interpreting with suspicion — and without paranoia

Legitimate COAs contain unresolved detail. Where the same COA reads like a template, it usually is one — and a template is not fraud, it is laziness. It becomes a problem when it is the only evidence you have.

Weak structural signals, in rough order of seriousness:

SignalWhy it matters
No batch number, or a batch number that changes between documentsBreaks the link between document and goods entirely
No test method statedResult cannot be interpreted or compared
No specification limitsPass/fail cannot be assessed
Results given as ranges or as "typical"Not a measurement of your batch
Issuing laboratory unnamedCannot assess accreditation or independence
All parameters at exactly the same value across batchesPhysically implausible; suggests templating
Issue date before the production dateInternally contradictory
Certificate held by a group parent, goods supplied by another entityChain of accountability is unclear, though not necessarily wrong
Missing parameters your specification requiresThe document was written to a different specification than yours

None of these individually makes a supplier dishonest. Collectively, they tell you the level of documentation maturity you are dealing with — which is a legitimate and useful thing to know, because it predicts how the rest of the project will go.

What a COA cannot tell you

This is the part that matters most, and it is the part buyers skip.

A COA is not evidence of identity. It reports testing against a specification. Whether the material in the bag matches the material the COA describes is a sampling question, and sampling is not something a certificate can solve for you.

A COA is not evidence about your batch unless the batch number matches. Covered above, worth repeating.

A GMP certificate is not a product claim. GMP status concerns systems and processes at a site. It says nothing about whether a given lot of material is what it claims to be. A site can hold impeccable certifications and still be sent a substituted raw material by an upstream supplier.

FDA does not certify facilities or laboratories. A common industry misconception: FDA registers facilities that manufacture, process, pack or hold food for U.S. consumption, and it inspects them. It does not issue GMP certificates. When you see "FDA certified" on a COA or a website, ask what specifically is registered, in whose name, and for what activity. The answer is often legitimate — but it is usually not what the claim implies.

COAs are not comparable across suppliers by default. Two documents both showing "≥95%" may be measuring different things, by different methods, with different sampling. Normalising the specification is what makes comparison possible.

A practical verification procedure

  1. Write your own specification first. Define parameters, methods and limits before you request quotes. Send it to suppliers as your requirement, rather than accepting theirs.
  2. Require method and limit disclosure. Ask for the full specification sheet, not just the COA.
  3. Ask which laboratory issues the COA, and whether it holds accreditation for each method you rely on.
  4. Request the COA for your batch number, or agree explicitly when it will be issued and what releases payment.
  5. Cross-read the whole document set. Specification sheet, COA, packing list and invoice should describe the same material, the same units and the same batch.
  6. Decide your independent testing scope in advance. For high-value or high-risk materials, commission testing from a laboratory you chose, on a sample you controlled the chain of custody for. This is the only route that answers the identity question.
  7. Record the gaps in writing. A short email listing what you could not verify is worth more than a feeling of having checked.

The point

The value of a COA is not that it exists. It is that it connects a measurement to a batch, by a stated method, from a laboratory you can assess, against a limit you defined.

That connection is what makes the document auditable. Without it, you have a piece of paper that looks like evidence — and in supplement sourcing, the gap between looking like evidence and being evidence is exactly where projects fail.
