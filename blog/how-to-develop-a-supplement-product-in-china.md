Most supplement projects in China do not fail at the factory.

They fail earlier — in a sequence of decisions made before anyone was asked to quote, each of which quietly narrowed the options available later. The formula that cannot hold its dose. The dosage form that has a 50,000-unit MOQ when the plan assumed 5,000. The active that turns out to be a Novel Food in the target market after the label has been designed.

None of this shows up in a quotation. All of it shows up in the invoice, the timeline, or the launch.

This is the development sequence we work through with brands, and the decisions in it that are genuinely expensive to reverse.

## The sequence, and why the order matters

Development runs in one direction only. Each stage constrains the next, and going backwards costs money.

**Target effect → Target consumer → Dosage form → Dose & actives → Specification → Manufacturer → Sample → Production**

Almost every avoidable problem we see is a stage that was skipped and then revisited out of order. The most common is choosing a dosage form first, because it is the part that feels concrete.

### Start from the effect, not the format

"What format should we use?" is the wrong first question, because it has no fixed answer. A better one: *what does the consumer have to experience for this product to work?* Once that is settled — a scoop in a shaker, a capsule in the morning, something that dissolves under the tongue — the format list narrows on its own.

The format then has to survive three tests:

- **Does the dose physically fit?** A 3,000 mg botanical blend does not go in a single capsule. It goes in a powder, a sachet, or several capsules per serving — which changes the cost per dose and the pack size the consumer sees.
- **Does the active survive the process?** Heat, moisture, pH and shear destroy different ingredients at different points. A gummy cooks; a probiotic does not enjoy that. A soft chew's water activity matters if you want live cultures to survive shelf life.
- **Does the format fit the market?** Some formats carry regulatory implications that others do not, and in the EU the classification question frequently bites at this stage rather than later.

### Decide the dose per unit early, and write it down

This is the single most useful number in the whole project. Until dose per unit is fixed, nothing downstream is comparable: not quotes, not specifications, not MOQ, not cost per serving.

A manufacturer cannot quote honestly without it, and a quote given without it is not comparable to any other quote — which is exactly why so many ingredient quotations turn out to be incomparable once you place them side by side.

### Then choose the actives, and be specific about form

"Magnesium" is not a specification. Magnesium citrate, magnesium glycinate and magnesium oxide carry different elemental content, different bioavailability, different taste and different cost. Named this loosely, they will be interpreted loosely.

The parameters that need fixing for each active:

- **Identity** — the exact ingredient, with its form (salt, chelate, extract, whole powder)
- **Assay or content** — what is actually measured, and against what
- **Standardisation** — for botanicals, whether it is standardised and to which marker
- **Extract ratio** — where relevant, and understood for what it does and does not tell you
- **Carrier and excipients** — a "90% extract" is 10% something else, and that something else matters
- **Particle size and flow** — irrelevant in a capsule, decisive in a sachet line

Two extracts with the same name and the same headline ratio can behave completely differently in production. The specification is what makes them comparable; without it you are buying a category, not an ingredient.

### Settle what the label is allowed to say, before design

Claims drive formulation, not the other way round. If the claim you want is not permitted in your target market, you want to discover that in week two, not after the artwork is approved.

This is where market-specific work belongs:

| Market | What to settle at this stage |
|---|---|
| **United States** | Structure/function claim wording and the disclaimer; whether the ingredient is a lawful dietary ingredient; NDI considerations for anything novel |
| **European Union** | Novel Food classification before anything else; permitted ingredient lists; claim wording from the authorised register |
| **United Kingdom** | Post-Brexit divergence from EU position — check separately, do not assume |
| **Australia / NZ** | Whether the product is a therapeutic good or a food; permitted forms and ingredients differ |
| **Canada** | NPN application path, permitted claims and the evidence expected for them |

The common failure in the EU is sequence: a brand confirms an ingredient, builds a formula around it, designs packaging — and only then asks about Novel Food status. If the answer is unfavourable, most of that work is wasted.

### Choose the manufacturer against the specification, not the price

A manufacturer is only suitable in relation to a specific product. The same plant can be the right answer for one brief and a poor answer for another, at the same quality level.

What actually determines fit:

- **Process capability** — can the line produce *your* dose and *your* format reliably, not merely "capsules"
- **MOQ** — and whether it is fixed by the line, the raw material, or the tooling
- **Certification** — what is held, in whose name, and what scope it actually covers
- **Specification tolerance** — whether the plant can hit the tolerance your specification assumes
- **Packaging and finishing** — in-house or outsourced, and to whom
- **How they communicate during a problem** — the single best predictor of how the next twelve months will go

Cost belongs in this list, but it sorts last for a reason. The cheapest capable manufacturer is worth far more than the cheapest manufacturer, and the difference only appears once production starts.

### Sample deliberately, and treat approval as a formal step

A sample is evidence about a sample. It is not evidence about your production run.

Two things make the difference:

**Sample the way you will produce.** Ask explicitly whether the sample will be made on the production line or in the lab, and at what batch size. A hand-made pilot is useful for flavour; it says nothing about fill weight consistency at scale.

**Approve against the specification.** Sign off on a documented sample — retained, sealed, dated, with the specification it was made to. Everything that follows is then measured against a reference instead of a memory. When you later need to argue that a production batch differs, this is the document that does the arguing.

### Production is where the specification becomes enforceable

Before the run: confirm the batch number, the raw material lots, the release criteria and who signs off. During: agree what you will be told and when — many projects drift simply because nothing was scheduled. After: retention samples and the QC documentation you contracted for, in the form your market requires.

The paperwork is not administration. In most disputes it is the entire substance of the case.

## The decisions that are actually expensive to reverse

| Decision | Cost of changing it later |
|---|---|
| Dosage form | High — tooling, MOQ, packaging, and often the regulatory position |
| Dose per unit | High — reformulation, new sample, new specification, new MOQ |
| Active form (salt, chelate, extract) | High — specification, labelling and cost all move |
| Specification parameters | Medium–high — comparability with existing quotes is lost |
| Target market | High if discovered late — EU Novel Food in particular |
| Manufacturer | Medium — depends on how much tooling and packaging is committed |
| Packaging format | Medium — artwork, fill line and freight all change |
| Label wording | Low — if the claim was checked early; high if it was not |

Read that table as a development order. The further down the list you are, the cheaper it is to change your mind — which is why the cheap decisions belong last.

## What brands most often get wrong

**Treating the quotation as the analysis.** A quotation is an output. It cannot tell you whether the specification behind it was complete, and it is not a substitute for having one.

**Comparing prices across non-identical briefs.** This is the most expensive mistake on the list, because it produces confident decisions from meaningless data. If two quotes were based on different specifications, they are not two prices for one thing.

**Optimising ingredient cost before the formula is settled.** Ingredient substitution changes taste, texture, stability and often the specification — so the saving is frequently reversed by the next sample round.

**Leaving the manufacturer out of the development conversation.** The plant sees problems your formula has not yet encountered. Early input from a capable manufacturer is one of the cheapest forms of quality control available.

**Assuming sample approval covers production.** It does not. It covers the sample.

## How this runs with someone on the China side

On our projects the sequence above is the whole job: fix the specification, screen manufacturers against it, run the sample round with documented references, then manage the run and the documentation.

That is not because the process is complicated. It is because each stage needs someone to hold the line on it — and because the decisions that matter are all made before the first purchase order, in conversations that happen in Mandarin, on the phone, at the plant.

If you are at the start of that sequence with a product concept, or in the middle of it with a formula and no manufacturer yet, that is the conversation worth having.
