How to Verify a China Supplement Manufacturer Before Paying

16 September 2026 · 9 min read · Jun Lee

A practical due-diligence sequence for supplement brands — what to check about the legal entity, the production site, licences, documentation and capability before any deposit leaves your account.

Most supplement sourcing disasters are not caused by a supplier who lied convincingly. They are caused by a buyer who never asked a question precise enough to be lied to about.

That is not a criticism of buyers. It is a description of the information asymmetry. When you are sourcing from another country, in another language, against documents that look official and complete, the natural instinct is to evaluate what you can see — a profile, a catalogue, a certificate, a price — and to treat the absence of a red flag as a green light.

Verification is the deliberate replacement of that instinct with a sequence. This article lays out the sequence, in the order that removes the most risk for the least effort.

Why the sequence matters more than the checklist

A common mistake is to treat supplier verification as a checklist to complete. It is not. Each finding changes what the next step should be.

If you establish that the entity quoting you is not the entity that holds the production licence, the question is no longer "are they a factory" but "what exactly is their relationship to the operation, and who is accountable if something goes wrong". If you establish that the dosage form you need sits in an in-house line, the documentation question becomes narrower and more answerable.

So work in layers, and let each layer decide the next.

Layer 0 — Define what you are actually buying

This sounds trivial. It is the layer most often skipped, and it makes every later layer unanswerable.

Before you contact anyone, write down — for your own internal use, not for the supplier:

  • the ingredient or formula, with dose per unit and target standardisation
  • the dosage form and unit size
  • the batch size you actually need, and the volume you expect over 12 months
  • the market you are selling into
  • which documents you will need for that market
  • what you are willing to be flexible on, and what you are not

Without this, you cannot evaluate a quotation, because you have nothing to compare it against. A supplier cannot be "capable" or "incapable" in the abstract. Capability is always capability for a specific product, at a specific volume, to a specific market.

Layer 1 — Identify the legal entity

Start with the company, not the product.

In China, the basic public record is the business licence (营业执照), which carries the Unified Social Credit Code (统一社会信用代码) — the 18-character identifier used across registries. The National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (国家企业信用信息公示系统, gsxt.gov.cn) is a public search that can be used to look up registration data.

What you are trying to establish:

CheckWhy it matters
Does the legal name on the quotation match the name you will pay?A bank transfer to a different entity than the contracting entity weakens your position enormously if a dispute arises
Is the registered business scope broad enough to cover what they claim?Registered scope is not proof of capability, but a mismatch is a question worth asking
Is the registered address the same as the claimed production address?Different addresses are normal — head offices exist — but the reason should be explainable
How old is the registration, and has the capital been changed recently?Rapid changes around the time of your order are a prompt to look closer, not an automatic problem
Is the entity you are dealing with a subsidiary, a branch, or an unrelated company?Group structures are common and legitimate; unstated ones are a communication hazard

None of these individually proves anything. Collectively they tell you whether the story you have been told is internally coherent.

Layer 2 — Establish where production physically happens

This is the layer that answers the "are they actually a factory" question, and it deserves its own treatment — we cover it in detail in How to tell if a Chinese supplier is actually a factory.

The short version: you are looking for evidence about process, not about identity. A price list tells you nothing. Questions do.

Ask about process parameters that only someone close to production would know without looking them up:

  • What is your blending capacity per batch for this dosage form?
  • Which line would this run on, and what else runs on that line?
  • How do you handle a raw material that fails incoming QC?
  • What is your typical yield loss on this format, and what causes it to vary?
  • Which steps in this process do you do in house, and which do you subcontract?

Smooth, specific, slightly boring answers are a good sign. Vague enthusiasm is not.

Layer 3 — Scope of licences and certifications

Here is where a lot of buyers go wrong, in both directions.

Certifications are not interchangeable. A food production licence (食品生产许可证, carrying an "SC" number) covers food production categories. Health foods (保健食品) sit in a separate regulatory regime with their own registration or filing status. A plant may legitimately hold one and not the other. What matters is which regime covers your product.

Certifications belong to entities and sites, not to brands. When you are shown an ISO 22000, FSSC 22000, HACCP, BRCGS or similar certificate, read it for the certificate holder name and the site address. A certificate held by a group parent, or held for a different site, is not wrong — but it is not evidence about the site that will run your batch either.

FDA registration is widely misunderstood. The U.S. FDA does not "certify" facilities and does not issue GMP certificates. Food and supplement facilities that export to the United States register with FDA and are subject to inspection; registration is not an approval, and it does not attest to your product. Be sceptical of "FDA certified" as a claim in itself, and ask instead what is registered, in whose name, and for what activity.

Layer 4 — Documentation review

Request a document set and read it as a set, not as individual trophies. The question is not "is there a certificate" but "do these documents describe the same thing".

Ask for:

  • specification sheet for the finished product as it will be supplied
  • incoming specification for each active ingredient
  • a recent COA for each ingredient you care about
  • certificates in the name of the site that will produce your batch
  • allergen, origin, non-GMO or other declarations your market requires
  • the packaging specification and artwork approval process
  • a sample batch record format (even redacted) so you know what traceability looks like

Then check for internal consistency: does the COA's batch number format match the specification's stated format? Do the declared units match? Do the ingredient names on the COA match the ones on the specification? Does the certificate cover the site, or an affiliate?

The most common failure is not forgery. It is a document set assembled from different sources that was never meant to be read together — and reading it together is exactly how you find the gaps.

If you want to go deeper on certificates specifically, see How to verify a COA from a Chinese supplier.

Layer 5 — Capability fit against your Layer 0

Now bring it back to your own product. The question changes from "are they good" to "are they good for this".

Capability questionWhat a weak answer looks like
Dosage form"Yes we can do that" with no mention of equipment or minimum run
Dose per unitAgreeing instantly to a dose that strains the format
Batch sizeAccepting your batch size far below their normal range without comment
MOQMOQ that moves depending on how interested they are
Testing scope"All tests included" with no list
Lead timeA number with no dependency on your artwork or material approvals
Market requirementsNo questions about your target market before quoting

The pattern to watch for is frictionless agreement. Real manufacturing involves constraints, and a capable supplier will tell you about them because they want the project to run smoothly. A supplier who agrees to everything is either not close enough to production to know the constraints — or is planning to renegotiate later.

Layer 6 — Commercial terms that protect you

Verification continues into the contract. These are the clauses that convert verification into protection:

  • Payment schedule tied to milestones, not to dates. Deposit, post-sample-approval, pre-shipment.
  • A written sample approval protocol stating exactly what the approved sample is, what it was made on, and that production must match it. If the sample was made on a pilot line, say so in the document.
  • Testing scope named explicitly, including who pays for what test and at which stage.
  • Batch records and traceability as a deliverable, not a favour.
  • Artwork and packaging approval gates with a stated turnaround, because missed artwork approvals are one of the most common causes of delay that gets blamed on the factory.
  • Tooling and mould ownership, if you are paying for any.
  • Change control — anything that changes ingredient source, specification or process after approval should require your written agreement.

Layer 7 — Pre-shipment

Even a well-verified supplier is worth checking at the point of shipment, particularly on a first order. A pre-shipment check can confirm quantities, packaging, labelling, batch numbers and that the goods match what was approved. Where a question can only be answered on site, that is what a factory visit or a third-party inspection is for — and it should be scoped explicitly rather than assumed.

What verification does not do

This deserves to be stated plainly, because the value of a verification process depends on being honest about its limits.

Verification does not guarantee supplier conduct. It does not establish that an ingredient is chemically what it claims to be — that requires analytical testing against a specification, performed by a laboratory. It does not eliminate risk. It reduces risk and, more importantly, it makes the remaining risk visible so that you can price it, accept it, or walk away.

Anyone offering to remove all sourcing risk for you is selling something they cannot deliver. What you can genuinely buy is a better-informed decision.
