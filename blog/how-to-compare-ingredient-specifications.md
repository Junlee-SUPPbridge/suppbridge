An ingredient quotation is an answer. If you did not write the question precisely, you cannot tell whether the answer is good.

This is the most common failure we see in ingredient sourcing, and it is not a supplier problem. A brand sends the same loose description — "ashwagandha extract", "bovine collagen", "liposomal vitamin C" — to five suppliers. Five quotations come back at prices that differ by 300%. Someone picks the middle one, because the middle one feels safe. Nobody can explain what the price difference actually bought, and nobody finds out until the first production run or the first third-party test.

## The same words, different materials

A specification that says only *what* the material is leaves out everything that determines its cost and its behaviour in your product. Two suppliers can both truthfully quote "ashwagandha root extract" while describing materials that are not interchangeable:

| What varies | Why it changes the price | Why it changes your product |
|---|---|---|
| Extract ratio | 10:1 needs ten times the raw botanical of 1:1 | Determines how much powder you must carry per dose |
| Standardisation target | A named marker at a fixed % costs more than an undefined "extract" | Determines whether your label claim is defensible batch to batch |
| Test method | HPLC versus UV can differ by a factor, not a rounding error | Determines what your own COA will show |
| Carrier and excipient | Maltodextrin, dextrin, silicon dioxide all count toward weight | Determines your actual actives per capsule |
| Particle size and flow | Milling and sieving add process steps | Determines whether it runs on your filling line |
| Residual solvents, heavy metals, pesticides | Testing and sourcing discipline cost money | Determines whether it clears your target market |
| Origin and material form | Root versus leaf, cultivated versus wild | Determines both price and legality in some markets |

None of this is hidden. Every one of these items is a normal commercial parameter that a competent supplier will state if asked. The problem is that most first enquiries never ask.

## Write the specification before you ask for a price

The practical fix is to reverse the order. Send a specification, not a description. A specification that produces comparable quotes covers eight things:

**1. Identity and source form.** Botanical name, plant part, and whether the material is an extract, a powder, a granulation or a liquid. "Turmeric extract" is not a specification; *Curcuma longa*, root, extract is.

**2. The strength basis.** Ratio, standardisation target, or both — and if both, which one the price is built on. If a supplier quotes a ratio without a standardisation figure, ask what the extract is standardised to. If they quote standardisation without a ratio, ask how much raw material went in. A complete specification states both plus the analytical method.

**3. Assay method.** Not just "HPLC" but which marker, which column chemistry, which reference standard. Two laboratories running "HPLC" on the same sample can produce different numbers if the method differs.

**4. Excipient and carrier declaration.** What percentage of the material you receive is not the active. This single line frequently explains a price gap that looks inexplicable.

**5. Physical parameters.** Particle size distribution, bulk and tapped density, moisture, solubility, and flow characteristics. These decide whether the ingredient survives your dosage form.

**6. Limits.** Heavy metals, residual solvents, pesticide residues, microbial limits, and any market-specific restriction. State the limit and the method, not just "passes".

**7. Documentation required.** Certificate of analysis per batch, allergen statement, non-GMO statement, halal or kosher certification if you need it, and the identity of the testing laboratory. Decide before you buy whether you will accept a supplier's in-house COA or require third-party verification.

**8. Commercial frame.** Quantity, delivery basis, packaging, lead time, and whether the price is quoted against the specification you wrote or against a looser one.

## The questions that reveal the answer

Once you have a specification, the quotation becomes readable. A few questions separate a supplier who controls their material from one who resells it:

> **"Which of these parameters do you control in-house and which do you buy to?"**

A supplier who standardises and tests in-house will answer specifically about their process. A reseller will answer about their supplier.

> **"Can you send the specification you would quote against, in your own document format?"**

This is the single most useful request in ingredient sourcing. What comes back tells you how precisely the supplier thinks — and whether their spec matches yours or quietly substitutes an easier one.

> **"What lot-to-lot variation should we expect on the standardisation figure?"**

A range is a sign of a real answer. "It is always consistent" is a sign that nobody measured.

## Where the price difference actually lives

When two quotations for apparently the same ingredient differ sharply, the gap is almost always in one of four places, in roughly this order of frequency:

**Standardisation and assay.** A material standardised to a named marker at a stated percentage is a different product from an unstandardised extract of the same plant. This is the largest single driver and the one most often left unstated.

**Carrier loading.** If one quotation is materially cheaper and the specification says nothing about excipients, assume a different carrier ratio until proven otherwise. Ask for the percentage, then re-quote against actives.

**Documentation and testing scope.** Full heavy-metal, pesticide and solvent panels plus third-party verification cost real money. A cheaper quote may simply exclude them. This is not necessarily a problem — it is a problem when you assumed they were included.

**Scale and origin.** Volume pricing and raw-material origin are legitimate variables. They are also the two that suppliers volunteer most readily, which is why they are the two most often mistaken for the whole explanation.

## What to do when you cannot settle a specification

Sometimes you genuinely cannot define the specification before you enquire — you do not know what the market offers, or what the cost structure looks like. In that case, ask for the specification rather than the price.

Request: *"Send me the specification of the material you would supply for this application, including standardisation, assay method, carrier and the limits you hold, and the indicative price for the quantity below."*

You will receive several specifications that are not comparable. That is the point. Read them against each other, take the parameters that matter for your product, and write one specification from the union. The second round of quotations is the first round that means anything.

---

**The principle underneath all of this:** an ingredient has no single price. It has a price for a defined material, from a defined source, tested to a defined method, documented to a defined standard, at a defined volume. Until those five things are written down, you are not comparing quotations — you are comparing letterheads.
